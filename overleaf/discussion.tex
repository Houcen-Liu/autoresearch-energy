\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{RQ1: what the factor effects mean}

\textbf{Sparsity did not impose the trade-off it is usually assumed to impose.}
The registered mechanism for H$^{\mathit{prop}}$ anticipated a tension: MoE
sparsity lowers $E_{\mathit{prop}}$ per call, but a worse-steering proposer could
inflate iterations-to-kept-mutation and spend the saving back on training energy.
That tension did not materialise. The MoE was cheaper on every energy measure and
\emph{also} more accurate, so the net effect is not a compromise between the two
but an improvement on both. For a self-hoster choosing between these models on a
20\,GB card the decision is therefore not a trade-off at all.

The mechanism is multiplicative rather than additive. Activating $\sim$3B parameters instead of 14.8B lowers
instantaneous power by $\sim$36\,\% (72.5 vs.\ 112.8\,W) \emph{and} halves
proposal latency. Either alone would give a modest saving; together they give
2.6$\times$. This also explains why the effect is larger in $E_{\mathit{prop}}$
($-62\,\%$) than in $E_{\mathit{total}}$ ($-42\,\%$): training energy is common to
both arms and dilutes the architectural difference. Any design that lets the
proposer occupy a larger share of session time, a shorter training budget, a
larger loop budget, will show a larger architectural effect, which is why the
45\,s budget was chosen (Section~\ref{sec:execution}).

\textbf{Reliability, not mean accuracy, is where the arms differ most.} Best
accuracy observed per session averages $-2.21$\,pp for the dense proposer against
$+7.23$\,pp for the MoE, yet the dense \emph{median} is $+4.69$\,pp. The mean is
dragged below baseline by a minority of sessions in which proposals collapse to
chance level, one replaced SGD with AdamW while retaining a learning rate an
order of magnitude too large for Adam. The distinction matters practically: a
session ending below its own starting point has spent its entire energy budget
for a negative result, and the dense proposer did this 5 times in 12 against the
MoE's 1. Reporting only mean accuracy would have understated the difference
between the two arms and mischaracterised its nature.

\textbf{Patience behaved as if the mechanism were absent.} H$^{\mathit{pat}}$
posited a waste-versus-quality trade-off: greedy reverts noise quickly but may
discard real improvements, while patience tolerates regressions and may recover
changes that only pay off in combination. Neither half appeared (Cliff's
$\delta = 0.04$ and $0.12$, both $p_{\mathit{holm}} = 1.000$). The most plausible
reading is that the proposals are not compositional in the way the mechanism
assumes: proposals rewrite substantial portions of the recipe rather than making
small orthogonal changes, so a chain of provisional keeps rarely combines into
something better than its parts. A workload eliciting smaller, more local edits
might well show the effect the hypothesis predicted.

\textbf{Loop budget confirmed diminishing returns, and locates them early.}
Doubling the budget raised energy per retained improvement by 108\,\%
($\delta = -0.80$). Because the improvements that survive are found early, the
second ten iterations largely purchase rejected proposals. This is the one
contrast that survived Holm correction, and it carries the clearest practitioner
implication: for this workload, running a session twice as long is close to twice
as expensive per useful result.

\subsection{RQ2: reading the frontier}

Two of three non-dominated cells are MoE cells, and the cheapest, MoE, greedy,
budget 10, reaches 0.8209 test accuracy for 56.5\,kJ. As configuration
guidance: \emph{at any energy budget above 56.5\,kJ, the most accurate
configuration available is an MoE configuration}; the dense arm never buys
accuracy that the MoE cannot buy more cheaply.

The dense frontier point deserves scepticism rather than interpretation. It is
non-dominated only because its mean accuracy exceeds the cheapest MoE cell by
0.053\,pp, which is 23 times smaller than the measured keep threshold and 60
times smaller than the within-cell standard deviation, at 1.91$\times$ the energy.
It is an artifact of computing a frontier from cell means without reference to the
noise floor. We report it rather than silently dropping it, because the general
point applies to any Pareto analysis over noisy measurements: \textbf{a frontier
margin smaller than the measurement noise is not a trade-off, and a frontier
figure without a noise reference invites over-reading.}

\subsection{The harness is part of the instrument}

Three times during execution the experiment was floored not by any property of
the models but by information the agent could not observe: that the data was
already normalised, that the installed library versions had removed the APIs it
was using, and how few epochs the wall-clock budget actually buys. Each was fixed
with comments alone, and each moved the crash rate substantially
(4/5 $\rightarrow$ 1/5; 7/9 $\rightarrow$ 0/10).

The third of those has an arm-specific effect we did not anticipate. Told that
the budget delivers roughly 43 epochs, the MoE stops proposing schedules for
longer runs entirely (0 of 82 \texttt{T\_max} literals above 60 epochs across
Stage~2b), while the dense proposer largely does not (19 of 20 in Stage~2a).
Supplying an agent with a fact does not make it use the fact, and a prompt
intervention validated on one model cannot be assumed to transfer to another.

\subsection{A diagnosis we made and had to retract}

The episode we consider most instructive is one where we got it wrong. Phase~1's
first three sessions retained no mutations, and we diagnosed a degenerate search
caused by sampling at temperature 0: under greedy selection the recipe returns to
the baseline after each rejection, so a deterministic proposer sees an almost
unchanged prompt and returns an almost unchanged answer. The proposals bore this
out, with 0.982 mean pairwise similarity and one byte-identical pair. We changed
temperature, restarted, and retained mutations immediately.

The diagnosis was wrong, and a later experiment was needed to establish that. At
$T=0$ against the current prompt the MoE retains mutations at its normal rate,
and temperature has no detectable effect on retention or accuracy anywhere in
$[0,1]$. What temperature does affect is repetition: 23 byte-identical proposal
pairs at $T=0$ against 2--9 above it. Repetition is real, and it is wasteful,
but it is not what stops a session from succeeding; one $T=0$ session produced
four distinct proposals out of ten and still retained two of them.

The original observation needs no mechanism at all. The three sessions were all
dense, which the shuffled run order produces 11\,\% of the time, and the dense
arm retains nothing in 42\,\% of its sessions regardless. We had diagnosed a
system failure from a sample that was consistent with ordinary variation in the
worse of the two arms.

Two things follow for practice. Diagnosing an experiment from its first few runs
invites exactly this error, because the run order is randomised precisely so that
early runs are not representative. And an intervention made on such a diagnosis
should change one thing: ours changed sampling and prompt content together, which
cost a further twelve sessions to disentangle. We report the sequence rather than
the corrected conclusion alone, because a study that only reports what survived
gives no information about how often its authors were wrong along the way.

\subsection{Reasoning substitutes for sparsity rather than compounding with it}

Stage~2a was motivated by a plausible expectation: that a reasoning pass would
catch the kind of error the dense proposer made repeatedly, and that the sparse
model's cheaper tokens would make reasoning affordable where the dense model's do
not. The second half held: the MoE reasons for approximately what the dense
model spends not reasoning. The first did not, and the interaction is more
interesting than either.

Reasoning materially improved the dense arm ($+6.0$\,pp pooled) and did nothing
measurable for the MoE. Whatever deliberation supplies, the sparse model already
had; buying both is buying the same capability twice, at 1.8 times the energy.
We would not have predicted the direction of this interaction. A study running
only one arm would have drawn the opposite practical conclusion depending on
which arm it chose.

The practical consequence is a ranking that inverts the intuitive one. Faced with
a fixed energy budget, a self-hoster should spend it on a sparse model and leave
reasoning disabled: that configuration matched dense-with-reasoning to within a
fifth of the noise floor while using a third of the energy. Reasoning is not
useless, since it rescues a weak proposer, but it is an expensive way to reach a
place the sparse model reaches for free.

That reasoning \emph{increased} error rates in both arms (dense
1.0~$\rightarrow$~4.3, MoE 3.3~$\rightarrow$~4.3) contradicts what we expected. Deliberation produced more ambitious proposals,
not more correct ones. Whether this is a property of these models, of this
prompt, or of code-generation under a short training budget, we cannot separate
with the data at hand.

\subsection{What the unplanned replication tells us}

Phase~1 and Stage~2a each contain a \emph{dense, greedy, budget-10,
reasoning-off} cell, executed days apart. Their session energies agree to
0.20\,\%; their test accuracies differ by 6.42\,pp. Read together, these say the
apparatus is stable and the agent is not.

This has two consequences we would carry into any follow-up. First, it is the
concrete justification for the design's pre-registered preference for effect
sizes over significance tests, with agent-side variance of this magnitude at
$n=3$, a significance test on accuracy is answering a question about sample size
rather than about proposers. Second, it suggests the two dependent variables
deserve different treatment: energy comparisons here are far better powered than
accuracy comparisons, so where the two disagree, the energy result is the more
trustworthy. A design with the same budget spent as more repetitions and fewer
cells would have produced tighter accuracy estimates, and we would recommend that
trade to anyone repeating this.

\subsection{Contract compliance is not proposal quality}

The guard rejected none of the dense proposals and 9.6\,\% of the MoE's, yet the
MoE produced the better recipes. The pilot showed the same dissociation in a
sharper form: a 4B model was rejected on four of six iterations and still gained
$+8.8$\,pp, while a 14B model complied perfectly and gained nothing. Benchmarks
that score agents on output-format compliance or tool-call validity would rank
these pairs in the wrong order. Compliance and quality should be reported as
separate axes.
